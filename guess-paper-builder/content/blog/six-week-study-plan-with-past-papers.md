> **Short answer:** Spend the first two weeks on the syllabus and repeated topics, the middle two weeks on timed past-paper practice, and the last two weeks on guess papers, weak-topic repair and full mock exams. Review every attempt, because the review is where most of the improvement comes from.

## Key takeaways

- Start with the topics that repeat most often in past papers, so early effort pays off.
- Practise with timed papers from week three onwards, not only after you finish the syllabus.
- Review mistakes within 48 hours, while you still remember the reasoning.
- Keep one full mock exam each week in the final stretch.

## Before you start: gather your materials

Collect the last five years of past papers for each subject, your textbook, and your class notes. Decide which subjects you will study each day, and set a realistic number of hours. Six weeks with four focused hours a day is more effective than two exhausted hours.

If you have many papers, use a repeated-question analysis to rank topics first. The method is described in [how to find repeated exam questions](/blog/how-to-find-repeated-exam-questions). It gives you a priority list for the first two weeks.

## Week 1: High-frequency topics

Study the topics that appear in most years, starting with the highest count. For each topic:

1. Read the textbook section and make short notes.
2. Solve the matching questions from the past papers.
3. Write one or two new questions yourself, in the same style.

Aim to finish the top third of your priority list by the end of the week. Do not move on until you can explain the core concept without looking at notes.

## Week 2: Remaining syllabus

Cover the rest of the syllabus, starting with topics that appear less often but are still in the exam. Keep the same routine: notes, past-paper questions, and one self-written question. Do a short revision of Week 1 topics every other day, so the early material stays fresh.

## Week 3: Timed practice begins

Switch to timed sections. Take one past paper section each day under exam conditions, then review it the same day.

The review matters more than the timing. For each question you missed, record:

- The concept you did not understand.
- Whether the error was a knowledge gap, a calculation mistake, or a misreading of the question.
- The correct method, written in your own words.

## Week 4: Guess papers and weak topics

Start using guess papers built from your repetition report. Attempt one full guess paper per week under exam timing. Between papers, spend time on the topics you missed most often. Make a simple tracker with topic, number of misses and the date you last revised it. Revise the top three weak topics every few days.

## Week 5: Full mock exams

Take a complete mock exam, with the real paper's timing and no breaks beyond what the exam allows. Afterwards, spend as long on the review as you spent on the paper. Check marking schemes where available, and note any question types where you lost marks through poor structure, such as missing steps in a numerical answer or a short answer that was too brief.

## Week 6: Consolidate

Do not learn new topics this week. Instead:

- Attempt one or two more mock exams under timed conditions.
- Revise your weak-topic tracker and your answer notes.
- Practise writing answers in the format the marking scheme expects.
- Sleep well before each mock, because tiredness affects accuracy more than one extra hour of revision.

## Sample daily routine

| Time | Activity |
|---|---|
| 1 hour | Review yesterday's mistakes |
| 1.5 hours | New topic or timed section |
| 1 hour | Solve questions from past papers |
| 30 minutes | Update your weak-topic tracker |

Adjust the blocks to your schedule. The order matters more than the exact times: review first, then new work, then practice.

## Common mistakes

- **Only reading notes.** Reading feels productive but builds recognition, not recall. Attempt questions instead.
- **Skipping the review.** An attempt without review teaches you little.
- **Practising only easy topics.** Your weakest topics need the most time, so schedule them deliberately.
- **Changing the plan every day.** Stick to the structure for at least two weeks before judging it.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is six weeks enough?**

It depends on how much material you have covered already. If you are starting from scratch, treat six weeks as an intensive plan and extend the first two weeks if needed.

**How many past papers should I solve?**

Solve as many as you can review properly. Five well-reviewed papers are more useful than fifteen papers that you only check against the answer.

**What if I fall behind?**

Keep the timed practice and move the remaining syllabus to a later revision cycle. Missing the Week 2 target is less harmful than skipping review.

**Does this plan work for board exams?**

Yes, the structure applies to most written exams. Adjust the number of weeks and the subjects to match your exam date.

## Summary

Prioritise high-frequency topics, cover the syllabus, switch to timed practice early, review every attempt, and finish with full mock exams. Consistency and honest review will do more for your score than long hours with no feedback.
