> **Short answer:** A good guess paper follows the real paper's structure, uses the topics that repeat most often in past papers, covers the textbook gaps that examiners have not yet tested, and includes an answer key. It is a practice tool, not a prediction of the exact exam.

## Key takeaways

- Start with the exam blueprint: sections, question types and marks, taken from the most recent paper.
- Fill the paper with high-frequency topics first, then add textbook coverage for topics that are not yet repeated.
- Keep the difficulty and length close to the real exam so your timing practice is useful.
- Always include an answer key and mark your attempts against it.

## What a guess paper is (and is not)

A guess paper is a mock paper built from patterns in earlier exams. It helps you practise the types of questions you are likely to see, in the format you will face on the day.

It is not a leaked paper, and it cannot tell you the exact questions. Treat it as a training tool. Its value comes from practice under realistic conditions, not from the questions being right.

## Step 1: Copy the exam blueprint

Open the most recent paper and note its structure:

- Number of sections (for example, MCQs, short answers, long answers).
- Number of questions in each section and how many you must attempt.
- Marks per question and the total marks.
- Time allowed.

Write this down in a small table. Your guess paper should match it, so that the timing and layout feel familiar.

| Section | Question type | Questions | Marks each | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | MCQ | 10 | 1 | 10 |
| B | Short answer | 8 (attempt 5) | 3 | 15 |
| C | Long answer | 4 (attempt 2) | 8 | 16 |

Use your own paper's numbers. The table above is only an example.

## Step 2: Select topics from the repetition report

Go through your repetition report from highest count to lowest. For each section, pick questions from the topics that repeat most. A common approach:

1. Put the top three or four high-frequency topics into the long-answer section, because they usually carry the most marks.
2. Use the middle-frequency topics for short answers.
3. Use the lower-frequency but syllabus-critical topics for MCQs, which cover more ground quickly.

Avoid copying a past question word for word. Change the numbers, the context, or the form of the question, so that you are testing understanding rather than memory.

## Step 3: Fill the gaps from your textbook

Repetition data only covers what examiners have already asked. Your textbook may contain important topics that have not appeared yet. Look for:

- Chapters that are in the syllabus but missing from every past paper you have.
- Topics that appeared once several years ago and may return.
- Definitions and key terms that are easy to test in MCQs.

Write questions for these from the textbook content, and keep them at the same level of difficulty as the real paper.

## Step 4: Balance difficulty and length

A guess paper that is much harder or much easier than the real one wastes your time. A simple check:

- Can you answer the average question in the time the real paper allows per question?
- Are the long questions similar in depth to the real ones?
- Does the paper include a mix of easy and difficult questions, not only hard ones?

## Step 5: Write the answer key

An answer key is what turns a practice paper into a learning tool. For each question, write:

- The model answer, in the format the marking scheme expects.
- The marks breakdown, if the paper gives marks for steps or points.
- A note on the concept, so you can check your understanding if you got it wrong.

If you use an AI model to draft the answers, check every key point against your book. Models can make errors, especially in numerical questions.

## Step 6: Attempt it under exam conditions

Sit the paper in one go, with a timer and no notes. Afterwards:

1. Mark your answers using the key.
2. Note the questions you got wrong and the reason (concept, careless error, or time).
3. Revise those concepts, then attempt a new guess paper a few days later.

Repeat this cycle over several weeks. The gap between attempts is where most of the learning happens.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Only using high-frequency topics.** This leaves you exposed if the examiner changes focus. Cover the syllabus over time.
- **Ignoring the blueprint.** A paper with the wrong structure teaches you the wrong timing.
- **Skipping the answer key.** Without it, you cannot tell whether your answer is right.
- **Treating the guess paper as the whole preparation.** Use it alongside your textbook, class notes and teacher feedback.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long should a guess paper be?**

Match the real paper's length and time. If the actual exam is three hours, a three-hour practice session is the most useful.

**Should I make more than one guess paper?**

Yes. Several papers with different topic mixes give you broader practice than one paper repeated many times.

**Can the guess paper include questions from past papers?**

It can use the same topics, but rewrite the questions. Reusing exact past questions reduces their value as practice.

**Is a guess paper enough to pass?**

It is one part of preparation. Combine it with a full reading of the syllabus, regular revision and past-paper practice.

## Summary

A guess paper is a practical way to rehearse the exam. Copy the blueprint, fill it with repeated topics, cover the textbook gaps, write a careful answer key, and attempt it under timed conditions. Then review your mistakes and repeat.
