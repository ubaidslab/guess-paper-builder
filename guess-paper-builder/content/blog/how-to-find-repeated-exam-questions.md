> **Short answer:** Collect five years of past papers for one subject, split each paper into individual questions, group the questions that test the same idea, and count how many years each group appears. Groups that show up in most years are the strongest candidates for the next exam.

## Key takeaways

- Repeated questions are the highest-return part of revision, because the same ideas tend to return across years.
- Good analysis needs three things: complete past papers, a consistent way of splitting questions, and a way to group rephrased questions.
- Doing this by hand for ten papers takes hours, and rephrased questions are easy to miss.
- A free tool such as Guess Paper Builder can produce a repetition report, but you should still verify the groups against your syllabus.

## Why repeated questions matter

Most students revise every chapter with equal effort. That is slow, and it leaves you guessing about what matters. Past papers reveal a clearer pattern. Examiners often reuse the same concepts, sometimes with new numbers, a new context, or different wording. A question that has appeared in four of the last five years is a much safer bet than one that appeared once in 2019.

Repetition is not the same as copying. The aim is to recognise the underlying idea so you can answer any version of it.

## Who this method helps

- **Intermediate and board students** who have several years of papers and a textbook.
- **Teachers and tutors** who want to see which topics examiners return to most.
- **Self-learners** without a coaching centre or study group to share notes with.

## Step 1: Collect complete papers

Gather the last five years of papers for one subject. Use the official paper or a complete copy, not a summary. If a year is missing, note it, because your counts will be out of a different total.

## Step 2: Split each paper into questions

Number each question and keep sub-parts together. Write the question number, the year, and the full text in a simple table. Consistency matters more than perfection. A table with columns for year, question number, topic and text is enough.

| Year | Q no. | Topic | Question (short form) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2021 | 3 | Kinematics | Calculate the final velocity of a body under constant acceleration |
| 2023 | 5 | Kinematics | Find the velocity after a given time for an object accelerating uniformly |

## Step 3: Group questions that test the same idea

This is the hardest step. Two questions are in the same group when a student would use the same concept, formula or method to answer both, even if the wording and numbers differ. Put them in one group and give the group a short label such as "Newton's second law" or "photosynthesis: light reaction".

Watch for these traps:

- **Same wording, different idea.** "Define osmosis" and "Define diffusion" look similar but are different questions.
- **Different wording, same idea.** "Explain why plants need light" and "Describe the role of light in photosynthesis" belong together.
- **Multi-part questions.** A question with parts (a), (b) and (c) may repeat only one part. Count the part, not the whole question.

## Step 4: Count the groups

For each group, count the number of distinct years it appears in. Ignore duplicate entries from the same year. A group that appears in four of five years is a strong candidate. A group that appears once may still matter, but it is a weaker prediction.

Rank your groups by this count. You now have a shortlist of high-frequency topics.

## Step 5: Cross-check with your book

Repeated questions are only useful if they are in your syllabus and you understand the concept. For each top group, find the matching section in your textbook and check that you can explain the idea in your own words. If you cannot, that is a gap to fill first.

## Step 6: Practise under timed conditions

Build a mock paper from your top groups and attempt it under exam conditions. Mark it using the official marking scheme where available. Track which groups you still struggle with, and repeat the cycle.

## Limits to keep in mind

- **Grouping is approximate.** Keyword similarity can miss a heavily rephrased question, or merge two questions that share many words. Always check the groups yourself.
- **Scanned PDFs may not work.** Text-based PDFs give the best results.
- **Repetition is a signal, not a guarantee.** Examiners change papers. Use the report to prioritise, not to skip topics.

## Frequently asked questions

**What counts as a repeated question?**

A repeated question is one that tests the same concept or method as an earlier question, even if the wording or numbers are different.

**How many years of papers should I analyse?**

Five years is a common and useful range. More years help if the subject changes slowly. Fewer years can be enough if the syllabus changed recently.

**Is a question that appears once worth ignoring?**

Not always. A single appearance is a weaker signal, but if the topic is core to the syllabus, revise it anyway.

**Can I do this without any software?**

Yes. A table and a few hours are enough for one subject. Software is most useful when you have many papers or several subjects.

## Summary

Finding repeated exam questions is a method, not a secret. Collect complete papers, split and group the questions, count the years, and check the results against your book. Use a tool to save time on the repetitive parts, and keep your own judgement for the final decision.
